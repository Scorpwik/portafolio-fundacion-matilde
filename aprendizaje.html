What did I learn?

Through the development of the 'My Body, My Health' project for the Matilde Foundation, I learned that the information gap is one of the most invisible forms of structural inequality. My key learning was not technical but methodological: I understood how to translate complex statistical data and reproductive rights into a visual language that is accessible, empowering, and free of stigmas.

Why was it significant?

This learning was vital because it allowed me to connect my career in Interactive Media Design with an urgent human need in the Ecuadorian context. By analyzing statistics on contraceptive use and the age of first intercourse, I realized that design is a functional necessity.

Design as Social Justice

During my service at the Matilde Foundation, I observed that misinformation is a structural barrier that perpetuates cycles of vulnerability. Developing the "My Body, My Health" project taught me that my role as a future professional is not only aesthetic but deeply functional and ethical. By analyzing the data comparison between 2018 and 2026, I identified alarming regressions in access to modern methods in the married population, dropping from 92.8% to 81.5% in women, and a trend toward an earlier age of sexual debut in women at 17.5 years. These figures are not just numbers; they are the reflection of an information gap that design must close.

Key insight: The icons we considered 'universal' were not. Colors had specific cultural connotations, and typography was not just an aesthetic choice but a real accessibility tool for women with varying levels of literacy.

The design process became an exercise in humility and active listening. I began researching user-centered design methodologies, applying participant observation techniques to understand how the mothers at Matilde House processed visual information. I discovered that standard medical terminology generated rejection and confusion; thus, my work consisted of "translating" those reproductive rights into an empowering visual system, free of stigma and easy to navigate. I learned that true excellence in design does not reside in technical sophistication, but in the ability to make complexity invisible to bridge informational distances.

Professionally, this experience redefined my vision. Before PASEC, I saw design as a commercial service; now I understand it as social capital. My goal with the monthly workshops and digital campaigns is to increase knowledge about bodily autonomy by 50% within 6 months, using a Pre and Post-test system to validate that the change is not just visual but cognitive. This project is proof that a designer can and must be an ethical interventionist in their community, transforming misinformation into citizen power.
